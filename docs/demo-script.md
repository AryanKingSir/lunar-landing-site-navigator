# Demo script

## 30-second video (6 shots of about 5 s)

### Shot 1: 3D view, playback running
- **On screen:** shadows sweep around the south pole.
- **Narration:** "At the Moon's south pole the Sun never climbs more than two degrees. Terrain decides everything."

### Shot 2: two sites in the summary strip
- **On screen:** Connecting Ridge and the Shackleton crater floor.
- **Narration:** "Pick any sites and dates. A ridge gets sunlight the whole month; the crater floor next to it gets none."

### Shot 3: sky view at Connecting Ridge
- **On screen:** drag time until "Earth" changes from "behind terrain" to in view.
- **Narration:** "The real skyline from NASA laser altimetry, with the Sun and Earth as they move."

### Shot 4: timeline
- **On screen:** the Sun, Earth and radio-link bars.
- **Narration:** "See every window for power and for talking to Earth over the whole mission."

### Shot 5: chip menu
- **On screen:** "Land a month later" adds a column with different numbers.
- **Narration:** "Launch slips? Compare the new date in one click."

### Shot 6: Share pressed
- **On screen:** "Link copied", then the URL.
- **Narration:** "Send the exact comparison to your team. Lunar Landing Site Navigator."

## Slide outline (7 slides)

1. **The problem.** South-pole missions live or die by a degree or two of horizon. The existing tools are powerful but slow to use for comparing scenarios.
2. **Why the pole is hard.**
   - The Sun skims the horizon.
   - Earth bobs with libration.
   - Ridges cast shadows for days.
   - Crater floors stay dark forever.
3. **Our approach:**
   - JPL ephemeris plus LOLA terrain horizons traced along the curved surface;
   - a DSN-aware radio link;
   - a simple power model.
4. **Live demo:** the tutorial path. Pick a site, watch Earth rise, open 3D, add Shackleton floor, land a month later, read the timeline, share.
5. **Validation:**
   - matches JPL Horizons to within 0.004°;
   - permanently shadowed floor at 0%;
   - far side never sees Earth;
   - seasonal swing at Connecting Ridge.
6. **Who it helps:**
   - planners re-checking dates and sites after a slip;
   - teachers and the public seeing why the pole is hard.
7. **Next steps:**
   - relay satellites;
   - higher-resolution terrain near chosen sites;
   - thermal limits;
   - more polar landing candidates.
